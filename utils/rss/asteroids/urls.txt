Physical character (diameter, triaxial dimensions, mass + stated precision, rotation,
spectral type, discovery, spacecraft visits, landmark firsts):

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_exceptional_asteroids
  -> raw/exceptional_asteroids.html
  Read section by section (parse.SECTIONS), not by table index: the page reorders.
  Caveats the page does not warn about, all handled in parse.py:
    - 'Largest by diameter' ends with five Kirkwood-resonance rows that are not bodies
    - 'Most massive' has a 'Total' row, and one row whose precision is literally '?'
      (532 Herculina) -- parse.py's sanity() refuses to let that become a density
    - the 'Class' column there is a Tholen SPECTRAL class, not an orbit class
    - 'Mean distance from Sun' and 'Semi- major axis' are rounded; SBDB's elements win
      (check_crosscheck reports the gaps rather than inheriting them)
    - the 'Spacecraft targets' table has no Dimensions column and writes the ellipsoid
      straight into the Diameter cell ("56 ×24×21" for Ida). Reading only the first
      number there makes the page look like it quotes a longest axis; parse.py reads
      all three, which is why its effective diameters then land near LCDB's (Ida 30.4 vs
      28.0, Gaspra 11.9 vs 12.2, Itokawa 0.31 vs 0.38) instead of 2x above them

Rail data (osculating elements at an epoch, spkid, orbit class, discovery block):

https://ssd-api.jpl.nasa.gov/sbdb.api?sstr=<obj>&discovery=1
  -> raw/sbdb/<slug>.json   (one file per body named by the wiki tables, 259 of them)
  Query forms: "1 Ceres" works; "(127546) 2002 XU93" is REJECTED with a 400, so
  fetch.py falls back to the bare designation and then the bare number.
  SBDB has NO physical parameters: ?phys_par=1 returns {"message":...,"code":...}.
  Element values are ROUNDED (~3 significant figures: Ceres a = 2.77 au, per = 1680 d),
  so the period is the better-authored quantity if the loader derives a by Kepler III.
  SBDB also answers a MALFORMED designation with a DIFFERENT object: the page's
  "2010 EQ169" (a provisional suffix never has three digits, and the row is flagged
  citation needed) resolved to 2010 Chebyshev (1969 TL4). parse.sbdb_identity_ok()
  refuses such an answer -- in fetch.py before caching, in parse.py before merging --
  so raw/sbdb/2010_eq169.json stays cached but is never used, and 2010 EQ169 has no rail.

Adopted physical parameters -- rotation period + reliability code U, effective diameter +
DiamSource, geometric albedo + AlbSource, taxonomic class, H and G, binary flag, spin
poles, orbital family -- for 34,967 objects rather than 259:

https://sbnarchive.psi.edu/pds4/non_mission/ast-lightcurve-database_V4_0.zip
  -> ast-lightcurve-database_V4_0.zip, five tables extracted into raw/:
     lc_summary.csv (one adopted record per object), lc_spinaxis.csv (pole solutions),
     lc_binary.csv, lc_npa.csv (non-principal-axis rotation), lc_familylookup.csv.
     The 117 MB of per-observation detail rows are deliberately NOT extracted.
  THIS ONE HAS TO BE FETCHED BY HAND: SBN Archive sits behind a Cloudflare JS
  challenge, so a scripted download returns an HTML "Just a moment..." page.
  raw/lcdb_pds_readme.txt (the bundle's own column reference) IS kept in-repo: it is the
  authority for the source-flag codes below, so nobody needs the archive to read them.
  The .zip and raw/lc_*.csv are gitignored; parse.py prints a note and falls back to
  wiki + SBDB when they are missing.
  What the flags mean (pds_readme tables 4.1.4.4/4.1.4.6/4.1.4.7) -- an adopted value is
  not necessarily a measured one:
    DiamSource C calculated from albedo and H | D derived from albedo and H | L from a
               details-table entry | S SIMPS | T thermal | W WISE | K AKARI
    AlbSource  A ASSUMED from the orbital group or taxonomic type | D derived from H and
               diameter | L/S/T/W/K as above
  Across the 144 bodies this corpus joins: diameter C:55 D:44 L:43 S:2, albedo A:82 D:42
  L:18 S:2, class A:79 S:52 T:12 L:1. So most LCDB diameters are arithmetic on H and most
  albedos are class guesses. LCDB is the best source for a PERIOD (and it says how
  reliable it is), not for a size. Bennu is the cautionary row: Diam 0.213 km and albedo
  0.20 (assumed, class S*) against a radar mean diameter of ~0.49 km and albedo 0.049.
  LCDB has no mass and no orbit.

Orbits + absolute magnitude for 1,580,227 minor planets (895,910 numbered):

https://minorplanetcenter.net/Extended_Files/mpcorb_extended.json.gz
  -> raw/mpcorb/mpcorb_extended.json.gz   (175 MB gzipped, 983 MB as text, GITIGNORED)
  The plain MPCORB.DAT carries the same orbits as fixed-width text with a PACKED epoch
  and packed designators (numbers over 99,999 become letter+4 digits: Q9337 is 269337).
  The extended JSON has those unpacked AND adds H, G, the uncertainty parameter U, the
  observation/opposition counts and arc length, the perihelion passage time and a
  dynamical class. mporb.py cuts records by brace depth so the file streams; json.load
  needs ~4 GB.
  What it is good for, measured against the 259 rows already here:
    - H: all 111 matched rows that carry no magnitude anywhere else get one (the corpus
      has H for 144 rows: 46 from the wiki, 144 from LCDB, 255 of 259 matched)
    - a dynamical class: MBA 84, Distant Object 91, Apollo 44, Amor 12, Aten 9,
      perihelion<1.665 AU 8, Phocaea 2, Jupiter Trojan 2, Hungaria 2, Atira 1 -- over the
      255 matched rows. 115 of them state no spectral type at all, and this is the only
      classification the corpus has for them.
    - orbit quality: U, Num_obs, Num_opps, Arc_years, so a weak rail can be refused
  What it is NOT good for: the orbit itself. 245 of 255 sit at epoch JD 2461200.5, the
  same epoch as our SBDB blocks, and where they disagree both solutions are weak (2016
  DF2: 10 observations over 2 days; 2005 VX3: U 8). The biggest gaps are 17% in a on
  2016 DF2 and 2005 VX3, 9% on 2006 LM1. So parse.py stores the record and the generator
  keeps the SBDB rail.
  The MPC's header asks that the datafile be redistributed with attribution; we commit a
  per-row extract, and every consumer says where it came from.

Stated radius + mass for the bodies the exceptional list leaves size-less:

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Solar_System_objects_by_size
  -> raw/objects_by_size.html   (parse via sizes.py)
  Seven data tables by class. All 91 MPCORB "Distant Object" rows have NO diameter in
  any of the other sources, and H alone cannot size them: the albedo spans 0.04 (a coal)
  to ~1.0 (Eris' frost), which is a factor 5 in RADIUS. This page states a radius for 63
  KBO/SDO/plutino/cubewano rows, 25 Trojans, 12 centaurs and the dwarf planets.
  Parsing traps, all handled in sizes.py:
    - the row link's HREF is the only place number and name are separated; stripping the
      tags concatenates them ("Huya38628")
    - a provisional designation like "2025 UC11" is NOT number 2025
    - the RADIUS UNIT IS NOT THE SAME IN EVERY TABLE: tables 0-5 are km, table 6 is m.
      Table 0 gives Radius TWO sub-columns (km and Earth radii), so the unit row is
      offset from the label row -- align by column index and Eris, Makemake and Haumea
      silently drop. Read the header text in document order instead.
    - "0.205 - 0.465" is a stated span, not a value with a stray dash

Diameter + orbit for the trans-Neptunian population (~1,050 rows):

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_trans-Neptunian_objects
  -> raw/tno_list.html   (parse via sizes.tno_rows)
  One big table: Designation, Discovery, D (km), Class, a(AU), e, i, q(AU), Q(AU),
  Remarks, Refs. This is the Kuiper belt population -- MPCORB has 91 Distant Object
  rows for our cohort and sizes.py has 63 with a stated radius, but neither gives a
  few hundred sized bodies at 30-55 AU.
  Not every row is numbered or has a diameter; the classical/KBO/SDO/plutino/resonant
  class column is the classification, and D is often a rough estimate (the Remarks
  column says so when it is a brightness-derived guess).


Comet ORBITS (4,666 rows, one per apparition):

https://minorplanetcenter.net/Extended_Files/allcometels.json.gz
  -> raw/allcometels.json.gz   (parse via comets.py; 200 KB gz, committed unlike MPCORB)
  One row per APPARITION, not per comet: Orbit_type (C/P/D/A/I), Perihelion_dist, e, Peri,
  Node, i, the perihelion date (negative years are real: C/-146 P1 is the Comet of 146 AD),
  and for modern rows an Epoch plus H and G. The traps:
    - NO nucleus size anywhere. H is the COMA's brightness, and G (usually 4.0) is the
      slope that turns it into a heliocentric-distance law. Applying the asteroid H->diameter
      formula to a comet is not imprecise, it is meaningless: Halley "sizes" at ~20 km
      against a measured 5.75 km. So a comet is only ever joined to a stated radius.
    - grouping on the part before '/' puts 3,735 unrelated comets under the key "C"; the
      MPC's identity is "67P" for numbered ones and "C/1995 O1" for the rest (comets.id_key).
    - a = q/(1-e) is not a number for the 2,351 rows at e == 1.0 or the 354 hyperbolic ones:
      C/2022 F1 (ATLAS) gives a = 373,777 AU. Refused by name, never clamped.
    - the size page names four of its comet rows instead of giving a joinable designation,
      and two of those are not what they look like: the Great Comet of 1811 is C/1811 F1,
      and Tsuchinshan-ATLAS is C/2023 A3 (C/2022 N1 is Attard-Maury).

Nucleus sizes for the GREAT COMETS (the only long-period sizes we hold anywhere):

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_comet
  -> raw/great_comet.html   (parse via comets.great_rows)
  One 100-row table: Designation, Name, Image, Dimensions (km), Total magnitude (M1),
  Maximum brightness, Perihelion date, Remarks. The traps, all three handled in comets.py:
    - Dimensions is a DIAMETER, not a radius. Proved by the page against the by-size page
      rather than assumed: C/1811 F1 is "30 - 40 km" here and "15 - 20" (a radius) there;
      Halley "11 km" here against 5.75 km there. Halved once, in the parser, so no consumer
      has to remember it.
    - The Designation column is editorial. The page labels Hyakutake "C/1996 B1"; MPC's
      C/1996 B1 is Szczepanski, and Hyakutake is C/1996 B2. Both have a 1996 perihelion, so
      the DATES cannot catch the wrong join -- only the names can, which is why a row from
      this page carries name_guard and build() refuses a join whose identity words do not
      overlap (NAME_STOPWORDS keeps "great"/"comet" from doing it for free).
    - Halley's apparitions are ~28 rows under designations the MPC never adopted ("P/1066
      G1"), each repeating the same 11 km nucleus. They have no MPC orbit row to join and
      are refused by name: the corpus authors ONE Halley, at 1P's elements.
      (And the page writes BC years with U+2212 MINUS SIGN, not a hyphen.)
